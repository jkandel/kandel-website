An editor early in my journalism career had a simple rule: a story doesn't get published without at least two independent sources. 

Even as newsrooms began paying closer attention to website traffic, that rule remained non-negotiable.

Today, [Google's official quality guidelines](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) ask whether content avoids "simply copying or rewriting" existing pages or whether it provides value and original reporting. The editor's rule was about verifying a story. Google's guidance also asks what value the story adds.

A newsroom story I heard years ago has a reporter responding to an editor who asked when his piece would be ready: "Do you want it now, or do you want it good?"

AI has made producing text easy. But how is that going? Are we outsourcing our brains and forgetting that good writing takes time? It takes time to think. It takes time to verify information, to read primary sources, and to think again. Some consider writing fulfilling and fun, a very rewarding and human experience. While generative AI can help us organize our thoughts and make sense of vast amounts of data, among other big ideas and small, we're using it to generate slop.

Another line from my journalism days is appropriate here: "If your mother says she loves you, check it out."

My journalism background taught me that a secondary source, like news, a book, or a scholarly article, is a launching pad for further investigation.

As [Poynter's media literacy standards](https://www.poynter.org/mediawise/misinformation-resilience-toolkit-libraries/what-makes-a-source-reputable/) emphasize, secondary sources lead you to primary sources: the court documents, raw data, original filings, or the litigator who was in the room.

Once you have the primary sources, the digging begins: read the coverage, ask questions, and look for what the daily story left unexplored.

When a state or federal government passes a major production incentive, for example, the real story starts. An entertainment attorney client I work with [pointed out](https://laist.com/news/arts-and-entertainment/california-post-production-tax-credit-federal-credit-game-changer) that caps are reached quickly and the economics shape where the work will go.

That's the next question worth asking. Beyond the basic facts: What happens now? Who's affected? What will it cost? What's left undecided?

Two independent sources got the story published. What made the story worth reading was the next question.
